1 · The block

A solid block that works out where its holes should go

This is a 20 × 10 × 10 mm block of steel. Its left face is clamped to a wall, and a 605 N load pulls down on a strip across the middle of its free end.

Most of that steel carries almost no load. Press Optimise and the computer will decide, voxel by voxel, which material to keep so that the block stays as stiff as possible while using only half of it. Nobody tells it where the holes should go.

Everything you see is computed live in your browser. Each iteration solves a finite-element model of the block, with 6,897 unknown displacements, and then updates the design. Nothing is pre-recorded, so the result changes if you change the settings.

The method is the piecewise constant level set (PCLS) method of Wei & Wang (2009). Its special trick shows up in the first few seconds of the run: holes can open up inside solid material. The next chapter shows why that is unusual.

The Load case switch at the top runs the whole tour on a twisted bar instead. Torsion is the case where opening holes inside the solid matters most, as chapter 2 shows.

You are on the torsion load case: the end plate is twisted by a 3.0 N·m couple. The best use of steel against twisting is a hollow tube, and the block has to find that tube from the inside out. The Load case switch at the top goes back to bending.

Drag to orbit the view, scroll or pinch to zoom. The pink flashes mark holes that nucleated inside the block; they are listed on the Nucleation log tab below the 3D view.

2 · Why is that hard, normally?

A classical level set can only move the surfaces it already has

A classical level-set method describes a shape with a smooth “height map” ψ (psi) spread over the whole box. Where ψ is above zero there is material, and where it is below zero there is empty space. The surface of the part is the contour ψ = 0, like the coastline on a contour map. To change the shape, the method slides that coastline: the Hamilton–Jacobi equation raises or lowers ψ at each point at a rate equal to the surface speed times the local slope of ψ.

Four standard ingredients of that machinery stop it from opening a hole in the middle of solid material:

  1. ψ is reset to a distance every few iterations. Moving the surface slowly distorts ψ, so it is regularly re-initialised: replaced by the signed distance to the current surface (here every 5 iterations). A distance field only rises away from the surface, with no dips inside the solid, and any dip the transport had begun to dig is wiped out at the next reset. As Dunning and Kim put it, starting from a signed distance makes it harder for holes to emerge away from the boundary.
  2. The speed is only defined on the surface. The sensitivity that drives the method says how much stiffer the part gets if its boundary moves, so it only exists on the boundary. To move ψ everywhere, the speed is copied outwards from the nearest point of the surface, a step called velocity extension. A lazy voxel deep inside the block therefore moves with the nearest surface, not according to its own strain energy: nothing gives it an incentive to become void.
  3. The transport equation itself forbids it. Even with those two steps switched off, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation cannot lower ψ at the bottom of a dip, where ψ is flat. Its upwind discretisation makes this exact: in one step no voxel’s ψ can fall below its lowest neighbour’s, so void can only spread into voxels that already touch void.
  4. The front is slow, and the stiffness only sees the front. For stability (the CFL condition) the surface moves by at most about one voxel per step, and a voxel’s stiffness depends on ψ only within about a voxel of the surface. All the action is confined to the neighbourhood of existing surfaces, and many codes update ψ only in that narrow band.

In 2D these rules make it impossible to create a hole. In 3D one loophole is left: two fronts eating in from opposite faces can meet and punch a tunnel through a thin wall. What can never appear is an enclosed cavity, a void inside the solid that touches no surface.

How people usually copeThey seed the starting design with holes (“Swiss cheese”), so that the answer depends on where the seeds were put, or bolt on a topological-derivative step that punches holes where they would help most. PCLS needs neither: every voxel owns its value and is pushed by its own local forces, so material can change phase anywhere in the design domain, not only at the interface (Wei & Wang 2009, §6.1).

Both methods solve the same finite-element model and are driven by the same stiffness force and the same volume constraint. What differs is how the shape is stored and moved: PCLS changes a number in every voxel, while the classical method slides its surface, at most half a voxel per iteration.

On this bending case, watch the classical block (on the right, or underneath on a phone). It eats in from every outer face and, where two fronts meet, punches one tunnel through the web. After its 300 iterations that leaves an I-beam about 8 % stiffer than the PCLS design (8.3 against 9.1 mJ), while PCLS nucleates holes inside, ends with two tunnels and stops by itself at iteration 189, once its design has settled. So nucleation does not guarantee a better answer: it decides which shapes a method can reach at all. The classical interior count stays at zero.

In torsion the shear strain, and with it the strain energy, grows with distance from the axis of the bar, so the core does almost nothing and the best use of the steel is a hollow tube. 3 · Meet φ

PCLS gives every voxel one free number

PCLS throws the height map away. Every voxel owns a single number φ (phi) with a simple meaning: φ = 1 is empty space, φ = 2 is solid steel, and anything in between is not decided yet. The dividing line is φ = 1.5.

φ is not a distance to anything. A voxel’s φ does not depend on where the nearest surface is, so any voxel anywhere can drift below 1.5 and become a hole. That is the whole trick.

One voxel: drag the marker to set its φ
φ = 1.620

Two formulas do the work. The well function K(φ) = (φ − 2)(φ − 1) is zero only at φ = 1 and φ = 2, so asking for K = 0 everywhere is the same as asking every voxel to pick a side. Its slope K′(φ) = 2φ − 3 sets the direction of the push: a voxel above 1.5 is driven up to 2, one below is driven down to 1, and 1.5 itself is a knife edge. The bottom strip shows that push as arrows.

The voxel’s stiffness follows ρ(φ), a straight line from 0.001 at φ = 1 (empty space keeps a tiny “ersatz” stiffness so the equations stay solvable) to 1 at φ = 2. That straight line is what the volume constraint counts, and φ = 1.5 is where the exported part is cut. The finite-element model uses a bent version that makes half-decided material a poor deal, for reasons chapter 8 explains, but pushing every voxel to one side is still the job of K.

A row of 40 voxels

Each voxel settles on its own, without knowing where any surface is. Poke the middle of a solid row and that spot becomes a hole by itself. In the 3D run the same thing happens, while the physics decides which voxels drift below 1.5 in the first place.

4 · The physics

Material grows where it is working hard

To decide what to keep, the optimiser has to know how hard each piece of steel is working. So every iteration it solves the elasticity problem: how does the block deform under the load? The block is split into 2,000 cube elements with a node at each corner, and the unknowns are the three displacements of every node, 7,623 numbers in all, of which the 726 at the supports are held at zero.

From the deformation it gets the strain energy density in each voxel: the elastic energy stored per cubic millimetre, like the energy in a stretched spring. Colour the block by it and you can see where the load really flows.

In bending the energy piles up along the top and bottom faces, most of all near the wall where the bending moment is largest, and around the loaded strip. The middle of the section, near the neutral axis, is nearly idle.

In torsion the shear strain grows in proportion to the distance from the axis of the bar, so the energy grows with the square of it: the outer skin works hardest and the core is nearly idle. That idle core is what the optimiser hollows out.

The optimiser uses a normalised version: each voxel’s pull divided by the average pull on the material, where every voxel counts in proportion to how much steel it holds. A voxel at 3 is working three times harder than the average piece of steel and gets pushed towards solid; a voxel at 0.2 is nearly idle and loses the tug of war with the volume constraint.

A detail straight from the research code: the energy it uses is the one the voxel would store if it were solid, F₀ = F / ρm, not the tiny amount a nearly empty voxel actually stores. Across a gap in a load path F₀ is enormous, which lets a nearly empty voxel there ask for its material back. The pull is F₀ times p·xp−1, the rate at which the penalised model of chapter 8 gets stiffer as the voxel gains steel.

How the equations are solved

The solver is a matrix-free conjugate-gradient method. The big stiffness matrix is never built. Instead the code sweeps over the elements, multiplies each element’s 24 × 24 stiffness (scaled by its ρ) by that element’s displacements and adds up the results, which is the same element-by-element pattern as the CUDA kernel in the research code. A 3 × 3 block-Jacobi preconditioner speeds it up. The status line shows the CG count: how many sweeps a solve takes, typically 90 to 210 here, rising as the design sharpens and the contrast between solid and void grows.

5 · Four forces

Every voxel sits in a tug of war between four forces

At each iteration every voxel’s φ is nudged by four competing terms. The stiffness term is divided by its average over the material, so that average is exactly 1 whatever the load or the steel, and the other three are measured in the same units. That is what lets a single time step (dt = 0.02) work for any problem. Switch any of them off and restart to see what it was doing.

What you should see, measured on this problem. With all four forces on, the run converges at iteration 189 with 0.25 % of voxels undecided, a surface of about 1,300 mm², two holes nucleated inside and a compliance of 9.1 mJ. Then:

  • Volume off: nothing is ever removed and the block stays fully solid, the stiffest possible shape (5.2 mJ) but using all the material. The volume never reaches its target, so the run uses all 300 iterations. Every other force is positive or neutral on average.
  • Piecewise off: no grey fog, because the penalised model of chapter 8 makes grey steel a poor deal on its own, as in SIMP. But 3.8 % of the voxels stay undecided, 9 % of the design voxels never leave the wider grey band of the convergence test, and the run never settles: it uses all 300 iterations for about the same compliance (9.1 mJ). The piecewise force is what finishes the job, driving every voxel all the way to φ = 1 or 2.
  • Diffusion off: the boundaries turn ragged. The surface grows by 70 % (2,210 mm²), 25 holes nucleate instead of 2, and the part ends up riddled with 47 tunnels and 31 enclosed pockets. It even converges, at iteration 168, and is a little stiffer (8.9 mJ), but it is a sponge that depends on the grid and could not be made.
  • Stiffness off: nothing tells the design where the load goes. φ sinks to an even grey just below 1.5, so the block seems to melt away in the 3D view, and material survives only next to the fixed plates, whose φ = 2 diffuses into their neighbours. By the end the clamped end and the loaded end are joined by nothing but void, the compliance is enormous (1.6 J), and the run never converges: it even settles once while disconnected, which clears λ₂ (chapter 8).

What you should see, measured on this problem. With all four forces on, the run converges at iteration 192 with 0.15 % of voxels undecided, a surface of about 1,700 mm², two holes nucleated inside and a compliance of 2.4 mJ: a closed square tube. Then:

  • Volume off: nothing is ever removed and the block stays fully solid (1.45 mJ), using all the material, and the run uses all 300 iterations.
  • Piecewise off: the tube still forms and stays mostly crisp, thanks to the penalised model of chapter 8, but 4.5 % of the voxels end undecided, and how much steel you get depends on where you cut, from 52 % of the box at φ = 1.25 to 47 % at φ = 1.75. It never settles and uses all 300 iterations (2.36 mJ). Chapter 9 shows why the cut matters when a design becomes a part.
  • Diffusion off: much the same tube (2.4 mJ, a surface only 2 % larger), but 16 holes nucleate on the way instead of 2, and the wall never settles within 300 iterations. A tube has long flat walls, so there is little perimeter for the smoothing to save; in bending it matters far more.
  • Stiffness off: nothing tells the design where the load goes. The steel collects in two slabs against the clamped and the loaded plates, joined only by grey just below φ = 1.5. The model counts that grey and reports 23 mJ, but the part you would export has nothing between the slabs: re-analysed, its compliance is enormous (771 mJ, set by the void’s ersatz stiffness).

6 · The whole algorithm

Two nested loops, repeated a few hundred times

PCLS is an augmented-Lagrangian method. The inner loop moves the design while the two constraints push with fixed strengths; the outer loop then decides how hard they push next time, raising the pressure until the volume is right and every voxel has picked a side. Before the loops start, the lab builds the voxel mesh, the supports and the loads, sets every voxel to φ ≈ 1.625 (plus a little random noise) and the fixed plates to φ = 2, and sets λ₁ = 0, λ₂ = 0 everywhere, μ₁ = 0.2 and μ₂ = 5. Then, exactly as the research code does it (chapter 8 explains why the model is penalised and how the run knows when to stop):

Outer loop, repeated until the design has converged, or until the budget of Nout × Nin = 15 × 20 = 300 iterations is used up:

  1. Set this outer iteration’s stiffness exponent: p = 1 for the first two, then 1.4, 1.8, 2.2, 2.6 and, from the seventh on, 3.
  2. Start from the current design: copy φ into the working field φold.
  3. Inner loop, repeated up to Nin = 20 times, and never past the budget:
    1. Re-impose the fixed plates on φold: supports and loaded plates stay solid, φ = 2.
    2. Turn φ into stiffness in every voxel: ρ = −c₁(φ − 2) + (φ − 1), clipped to [c₁, 1].
    3. Measure the constraints: the volume error G₁ = mean ρ − V₀, and the piecewise error G₂ = K(φ) = (φ − 2)(φ − 1) in every voxel.
    4. Set this step’s constraint strengths: λ̃₁ = λ₁ + G₁/μ₁, and λ̃₂ = λ₂ + G₂/μ₂ in every voxel.
    5. Penalise the model: with x = (ρ − c₁)/(1 − c₁), give the finite-element model the stiffness ρm = c₁ + (1 − c₁)xp.
    6. Solve the elasticity problem K(ρm) u = f with matrix-free preconditioned CG, starting from the previous displacements.
    7. Compute each voxel’s base-material strain energy density F₀ = ½ ueᵀK₀ue/h³, its pull D = p·xp−1·F₀, and the compliance c = fᵀu.
    8. Find the scale m: the average pull on the material, each design voxel weighted by its ρ and none counted above 10 m (the fixed point m = Σρ·min(D, 10m) / Σρ, by bisection).
    9. Compute the forces on φ: stiffness min(ρ′D/m, 10), volume λ̃₁ρ′, and piecewise λ̃₂K′(φ) with K′ = 2φ − 3.
    10. Take an explicit step: φ* = φold + Δt·(stiffness − volume − piecewise).
    11. Smooth φ* with one semi-implicit diffusion step (additive operator splitting, AOS, with the diffusivity taken from φold) to get φnew.
    12. Clip φnew to [0, 3] and re-impose the fixed plates.
    13. Check the design: the grey fraction γ, how many voxels crossed φ = 1.5, the change in compliance, and whether the part you would export joins the load to the supports. If all five tests of chapter 8 pass (p is final, the volume is on target, γ ≤ 1 %, the compliance has stopped changing and no voxel crossed), the design is settled this iteration; count how many iterations in a row it has been.
    14. This lab only: find holes that nucleated and count the topology. Log one row of variables.csv.
    15. If the design has been settled for 10 iterations in a row and its exported part is connected, it has converged: stop the whole run. If it settled while disconnected, clear λ₂ and reset μ₂ to 5 (at most three times) and carry on.
    16. If for 3 steps in a row no design voxel has moved by more than 0.01·Δt (the largest net force is below 0.01), leave the inner loop early. Otherwise set φold = φnew and go round again.
  4. Accept the design: φ = φnew.
  5. Update the multipliers from the accepted design: λ₁ ← λ₁ + G₁/μ₁ and, in every voxel, λ₂ ← λ₂ + G₂/μ₂, both clamped to ±5.
  6. Tighten the penalties: μ₁ ← max(0.9 μ₁, Δt(1 − c₁)²), but only if |G₁| shrank by less than a quarter since the last outer iteration; μ₂ ← max(0.7 μ₂, 0.5) every time.
  7. Stop if the design converged in step 3; otherwise go round again.

The budget counts iterations, not outer iterations, because an inner loop can end early, and it should stay the same however short the inner loops become. On the tour’s problems every inner loop runs its full 20 iterations, and the runs converge well inside the budget: bending at iteration 189, torsion at 192.

The default run uses the research code’s production settings: the time step, the penalties μ and their decay factors, the clamps, the tolerances and the stiffness continuation. β is 0.125, the web app’s current default, which matches the problem files’ 0.25 from before the diffusion fix (theory.md §9.1). Only the budget is smaller, 15 × 20 = 300 iterations instead of 25 × 50 = 1,250, so that a run finishes in a minute or two. The Sandbox has the full budget, and can also run without one until the design converges.

Smaller μ means a harder constraint, so the constraints tighten as the run goes on. The inner loop ends early once for three consecutive steps, and the run stops when the design has been settled for 10 iterations and is connected (chapter 8, theory.md §5.4–§5.6).

The floor on μ₁ (theory.md eq. 4.8a) is the stability limit of the explicit volume update. One step moves every voxel by , so, with the share of voxels whose density is not clipped, the volume error changes by

Below the floor the factor turns negative and the volume overshoots at every step; once it passes −1 the error grows, and the design flips between all void and all solid. A run of a few hundred iterations never lets μ₁ decay that far, but the Sandbox can run until the design converges, so the floor is set at the safe value itself. It is capped at the starting μ₁, so a smaller starting value set in the Sandbox is kept.

Deliberate differences from WW09, all inherited from the research code (theory.md §9.2): the normalised driving terms, as a volume fraction error, the smoothing sign of the diffusion term, hard projection of fixed regions, and the adaptive μ₁ decay with floors and clamps.

The classical level-set twin, for comparison chapter 2

It keeps steps 5 and 6 of the outer loop for the volume constraint only (without λ₂ and μ₂, with the fixed floor of 5 × 10⁻⁴ on μ₁ and with no convergence test: it always runs Nout × Nin iterations), and replaces the inner loop, step 3, by:

  1. Turn ψ into stiffness: ρ = c₁ + (1 − c₁)·clamp(½ + ψ, 0, 1), the voxel’s share behind the front.
  2. Measure G₁ = mean ρ − V₀ and set λ̃₁ = λ₁ + G₁/μ₁.
  3. Solve K(ρ) u = f; compute F₀ and c.
  4. Surface speed in every voxel: V = min(ρ′F₀/mean F₀, 10) − λ̃₁ρ′ + β·κ, with κ the mean curvature of the contours of ψ. Its stiffness is not penalised: its surface is already sharp.
  5. Velocity extension: every voxel takes the speed at its nearest point of the surface.
  6. Move the surface: two upwind sub-steps of ∂ψ/∂t = V|∇ψ|, sized so that the fastest point moves half a voxel.
  7. Every 5th iteration, re-initialise ψ to the signed distance from the surface.
  8. Find nucleated holes and log.

7 · Spotlight

Catching a hole in the act

A hole that starts inside the block cannot be seen from outside, so the lab watches for them. After every iteration it finds the voxels that have just turned from solid (φ ≥ 1.5) to empty, groups touching ones into clusters and checks each cluster:

  • It touches an existing hole: that hole grew. Not logged.
  • It touches the outside of the block: the surface was eaten away. Logged as a surface pit, because a classical level set can do that too.
  • It touches neither: a hole nucleated inside solid material. It flashes pink, is logged, and the view briefly switches to x-ray so you can see it.

Press Show on any entry of the Nucleation log tab to jump back to that moment. The history slider in the transport bar replays the whole run.

The readouts also count the block’s topology with the Euler characteristic. A fresh interior hole shows up as an enclosed cavity; once it grows out to the surface on two sides it becomes a tunnel.

8 · Connected and converged

In 3D, grey material has to cost stiffness

The finite-element model and the part you export are two different things. The model is analysed with every voxel at its own stiffness. The part is what is left when every voxel with φ ≥ 1.5 is kept as solid steel and the rest is thrown away. A run is only finished when the two agree.

They agree once every voxel has picked a side. Until then, grey voxels just under φ = 1.5 carry load in the model but are missing from the part. In 3D that matters far more than in 2D. On any grid a browser, or even a workstation, can afford, the members of a 3D design are only a few voxels thick, so a load path can end up hanging on a link of grey voxels. The straight-line ρ(φ) of chapter 3 gives such a link no reason to fill in or to break, and gives grey material in general no reason to leave. So PCLS makes grey material expensive in the finite-element model. That is what keeps the exported part connected, and what lets the run tell when it has finished.

Its exported part ends 5.5 % less stiff than its model says, and less stiff than the penalised run’s part (9.15 against 9.08 mJ), although it uses 5 % more steel. (Set the final stiffness exponent to 1 in the Sandbox to try it.)

What you should see, measured on the torsion bar. The load path stays whole all the way through, and the run converges at iteration 192 with the exported tube within 0.3 % of the model (2.375 mJ). Here the penalty is not what holds the design together. A closed tube has walls several voxels thick and no thin links, and without the penalty the run also stays connected and converges, at iteration 198. Its tube is a little stiffer (2.24 mJ exported) but carries 7 % more steel, 0.51 of the box against 0.477, which accounts for the difference. It is the bending case, whose loaded end hangs on members a few voxels thick, that needs the penalty.

Why a grey link survives in a linear model

A load that has to pass through a thin link sets the stress in it, whatever the link’s stiffness. With the straight-line ρ(φ), a link at density ρ stretches 1/ρ times as far as solid steel under that stress, so the strain energy the stiffness force uses, F₀, grows like 1/ρ². The thinner the link gets, the harder its own stiffness force pulls it back up. Just below φ = 1.5, the piecewise force pulls the other way, towards void. The two balance, and the link sits just under the threshold for good: it neither fills in nor breaks.

Nothing in the linear model prefers a solid member to a grey one, either. A narrow bar of solid steel and a wide band of half-dense grey have the same stiffness and use the same volume. Wei & Wang leave grey material entirely to the piecewise constraint. In their 2D examples, on fine meshes at 40 to 50 % volume, members are many elements thick and grey stays confined to their edges, so that is enough. On a 3D grid, with members a few voxels thick, load can pass through grey links and through a thin grey haze in the void, and the constraint resolves them far too slowly, and by position rather than by function: it pushes each voxel to whichever side of φ = 1.5 it happens to be on, whether or not the voxel is carrying load.

What the penalty does

With x = (ρ − c₁)/(1 − c₁) running from 0 at void to 1 at solid, the finite-element model is given the stiffness ρm = c₁ + (1 − c₁)·xp, the SIMP law. At p = 3, half-dense material has only an eighth of the stiffness of solid for half the steel. Only the model changes. The volume constraint, the exported part and φ itself still use ρ, and for a design that is fully solid or empty ρm = ρ exactly, so the penalty changes the path the design takes, not how the finished design is judged. The finished design is still a solution of Wei & Wang’s problem.

The stiffness force is the exact rate of change of the penalised model’s compliance: the energy F₀ times p·xp−1. Of two voxels side by side, sharing the same strain, the denser one is now pulled harder, grows, and takes the load off the lighter one, which drains. A grey band splits into a solid member and void. A link that carries load is pulled far past what the piecewise force can hold down, and fills in; a link that carries nothing drains away. Starting at p = 3 would freeze the design before its layout has formed, so p stays at 1 for the first two outer iterations, while the topology forms, and then rises by 0.4 per outer iteration to reach 3 at the seventh.

Why it is needed to converge

A run has converged when the design has stopped changing and is black and white. The run counts an iteration as settled when

  1. p has reached its final value, so the model itself has stopped changing;
  2. the volume is within 0.01 of the target (|G₁| ≤ 0.01);
  3. at most 1 % of the design voxels are grey, with φ between 1.1 and 1.9;
  4. the compliance changed by less than 0.1 %;
  5. no design voxel crossed φ = 1.5 (one in 10,000 may).

It has converged once it has been settled for 10 iterations in a row and the exported part is connected: every loaded voxel joined to a support through solid voxels that touch at a face, an edge or a corner, with at most 0.5 % of the solid off the load path. The Settled and Load path readouts under the 3D view show both tests live.

With a linear model the grey test is the one that fails. Grey links and grey bands cost nothing, so they stay, and on the bending block 4.8 % of the design voxels are still grey after 300 iterations: the run can never stop by itself. With the penalty, grey is a bad deal wherever it is, the design turns black and white, and the tests can pass. Asking instead for K(φ) = 0 in every voxel would never work. A solid voxel that carries load settles above φ = 2, held up there by its stiffness force, where K is up to 2, so that test could never pass.

Should a design ever settle while its part is disconnected, the multiplier field λ₂ is cleared, at most three times. That releases the double well that was holding the voxels in place, so a gap can refill and an unloaded stub can drain. With the penalty on, the tour’s normal runs never need it, because the part is connected long before it settles. Only chapter 5’s run with the stiffness force switched off, which has nothing to hold it together, uses it.

Two smaller rules go with this. The inner loop ends as soon as no design voxel is still being pushed: the largest step any of them takes, divided by dt, stays below 0.01 for 3 iterations. On the tour’s problems every inner loop still runs its full 20 iterations, because the last grey voxels keep moving until the end. And the budget is counted in iterations, outer × inner = 300 here, so it stays the same however short the inner loops become. The Sandbox can also run until the design converges, with an optional time limit.

An average pull on the material

The stiffness force is divided by a scale so that its typical size is 1. With the penalty on, a plain average over the box would be the wrong scale twice over. A fully void voxel has no pull at all, so an average over the whole box shrinks with the amount of steel, and the scaled force inside the structure grows with it. And across a gap in a load path the strain energy is huge, which would inflate the average and weaken the force everywhere else: the opposite of what a gap needs. The scale m is therefore the average pull on the material. Each design voxel is weighted by its density, and no voxel may count for more than 10 m. After the clip at 10, the material-weighted average of the stiffness force is exactly 1. The fixed plates carry the highest strain energy but are not free to change, so they do not set the scale.

9 · To CAD

A design that has made up its mind is easier to build

Generative design only pays off once its answer leaves the optimiser: as a CAD solid that an engineer can dimension, edit and check, or as a watertight surface for a printer or a toolpath. How much work that takes depends on what the optimiser hands over.

What SIMP hands over

SIMP (solid isotropic material with penalisation), the most widely used method, gives every element a density between 0 and 1 and makes grey uneconomical with a penalty: stiffness grows like density cubed. It also needs a filter over a few elements to stop checkerboards and mesh dependence, and together they leave a band of grey elements along every boundary. A density field is not yet a shape. To get one, someone has to:

  1. choose a cut-off density, a decision the optimisation never made (0.5 is customary);
  2. accept that the part changes: grey material that was counted fractionally is either kept whole or thrown away, so the volume, the stiffness and sometimes the topology shift, and thin members can vanish or break off;
  3. turn the element staircase into a surface, usually with marching cubes followed by smoothing, which moves the boundary again;
  4. rebuild that faceted surface as a CAD model by surface fitting or by remodelling it feature by feature, usually the slowest and most manual step of the chain (Subedi et al. 2020);
  5. re-analyse the rebuilt part, because none of the steps above is guaranteed to preserve its performance.

What PCLS hands over

PCLS finishes with a field that has already decided. The piecewise constant constraint K(φ) = 0 drives every voxel to φ = 1 or φ = 2, and the diffusion term keeps boundaries short and smooth, so well under 1 % of voxels are left undecided at the end (0.25 % on the bending case). The boundary is the level set φ = 1.5 of an almost binary field: in Wei & Wang’s words, the geometry is represented by the level set function, while the density simply assigns each region its material, in contrast to SIMP’s element “raster”. Down the chain above, that means:

  • No threshold to choose. Any cut between φ = 1.25 and 1.75 gives almost the same part. Drag the slider below: the PCLS part hardly changes, while a SIMP part grows and shrinks.
  • What you export is what was optimised. A run only stops once its model and its part agree (chapter 8), so re-analysing the black-and-white part changes the compliance by a fraction of a per cent. For SIMP the change is 20 to 30 %.
  • A clean surface to start from. The iso-surface of φ is smooth and watertight, with sub-voxel positions (try the Surface view), and the perimeter term has already removed checkerboards and tiny features. The research code’s conformal analysis meshes exactly this surface, with marching cubes, an alpha wrap and TetGen.
  • Topology you can check. Holes, tunnels and enclosed cavities are unambiguous, so they can be counted before export (the Topology readout). A sealed cavity, like the core of the torsion tube, cannot be machined and would trap powder in a printed part, so it is worth knowing about.
  • And it still nucleates holes. A classical level set also hands over a crisp surface, but it cannot create topology (chapter 2). PCLS keeps the topological freedom of a density method and the sharp boundary of a level set.

    About this demonstrator

    This page is a from-scratch browser re-implementation of the 3D PCLS topology optimiser in the generative-design/PCLS research code, with its penalised finite-element model and its convergence and connectivity tests. It was written for people meeting topology optimisation for the first time, but the numerics follow the production code line by line: the same element, the same update rule, the same multiplier schedule and the same stopping rules. Run against the production PCLS_Optimiser on five small test problems, with an exact linear solve on the Python side, it stops at the same iteration for the same reason every time, takes every discrete decision identically at every iteration (voxels changing phase, pieces, load path, settled count), and ends with design fields that agree to between 10⁻⁸ and 3 × 10⁻⁵ after 194 to 600 iterations (4 × 10⁻¹¹ after the first).

    What is the same as the research code

    • Trilinear 8-node hexahedra with the kernels' corner ordering, Voigt convention and 2×2×2 Gauss rule; linear ersatz material ρ(φ) with c₁ = 10⁻³; E = 210 GPa, ν = 0.3, 1 mm voxels.
    • Voxel masks smeared onto element corners exactly as in ConjugateGradient._volume_mesh, including dropping loads that sit on fixed DOFs.
    • Matrix-free, element-by-element operator with a 3 × 3 nodal block-Jacobi preconditioner, stopped at ‖r‖ ≤ max(10⁻³ N, 10⁻⁶‖f‖).
    • The full PCLS update of pcls_optimiser.py: the penalised model stiffness c₁ + (1 − c₁)xᵖ with p ramped from 1 to 3, F₀ = F/ρm recovery and the p·xᵖ⁻¹F₀ driver, the density-weighted winsorised scale over the design voxels with the [0, 10] clip, volume and piecewise terms, the symmetric face-centred AOS step with zero-flux ends, the φ clip to [0, 3], passive-region projection, adaptive μ₁ decay with its stability floor Δt(1 − c₁)², the other floors and the ±5 clamps.
    • The loop control: the largest-net-force inner exit, the settled test (p, volume, grey fraction, compliance change, phase switches) over a 10-iteration window, the 26-connected load-path check with the λ₂ reset, the iteration budget counted in iterations, run-until-converged and the time limit.
    • Logged quantities with the same 47 columns, names and meaning as variables.csv.

    What is simplified for the browser

    • The solver is plain JavaScript in double precision, running in a Web Worker, instead of the production PyPardiso direct solver, the CUDA float32 PCG or the conformal CGAL/TetGen pipeline. The element matrix is evaluated once rather than per Gauss point per sweep, which gives identical arithmetic results.
    • Each solve is warm-started from the previous displacement field (the production PCG starts from zero). Both converge to the same tolerance.
    • Grids are small, 12 × 6 × 6 to 32 × 16 × 16 voxels (20 × 10 × 10 by default) instead of 128 × 32 × 32, so designs are coarse and a few voxels thick.
    • The default budget is 15 outer × 20 inner = 300 iterations instead of the problem files’ 25 × 50 = 1,250; the tour’s problems converge within it. The full budget is available in the Sandbox.
    • The time limit counts the optimiser’s own computing time rather than wall-clock time, so a paused run does not use it up.
    • The classical level-set twin is a teaching comparison: first-order upwind transport with closest-point velocity extension and re-distancing every 5 iterations, driven by the same normalised forces. It is not a tuned level-set optimiser.
    • The SIMP twin is the textbook method (p = 3, a 1.5-voxel density filter, optimality-criteria updates) on the same grid and solver, not a tuned commercial implementation.
    • Surfaces are drawn with marching cubes for display only; there is no alpha-wrap or STL export.
